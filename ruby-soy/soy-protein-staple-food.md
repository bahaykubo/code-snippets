# Could Soy (and Corn) Feed the World if Redirected from Animal Feed?

## 1. The soy case

**Global soybean production (2024/25): ~427 million tonnes** — a record high, led by Brazil (40%), the US (28%), and Argentina (12%). Currently, roughly 60–70%+ of the crop is used as animal feed (soybean meal), with the rest going to oil, direct human food (tofu, soy milk, edamame), and biofuel.

### If 100% of global soy were redirected to direct human food

- **Calories:** ~427 Mt of soybeans (~446 kcal/100g) ≈ **1.9 quadrillion kcal/year**. Global human caloric need (~8.2 billion people at ~2,300 kcal/day) is ~6.9 quadrillion kcal/year. That's only **~28% of the world's caloric needs** — nowhere near enough to live on.
- **Protein:** ~427 Mt of soybeans (~36g protein/100g) ≈ **154 million tonnes of protein**. Global protein need is roughly 150 million tonnes/year. That's **~100%+ of world protein needs** — the strongest part of the case for soy as a feed-to-food shift.

### Why soy still wouldn't "feed the world" alone

- Humans need carbohydrates as the bulk of daily energy; soy isn't a calorie-dense staple crop the way grains are, so even total diversion leaves a massive energy shortfall.
- A soy-only diet lacks certain micronutrients (e.g., B12) — not viable as literally the only food.
- Much of the crop is already committed to oil extraction and industrial/biofuel use as a co-product of crushing, not purely animal feed — so "removing it from farm feed" wouldn't free the whole 427 Mt for food use in practice.
- Logistics, land suitability, and distribution/waste losses aren't captured in this idealized calculation.

### How much of a soybean can actually become human-edible food?

Composition of a whole soybean:
- **Hull (seed coat): ~7–8%** — fibrous, low-protein, typically stripped and diverted to animal feed as bulking filler. Essentially the only fraction not normally turned into human food.
- **Oil: ~18–20%** — refined soybean oil is already mainstream human food (cooking oil, margarine, salad dressing, shortening).
- **Defatted meal (protein + carbs): ~72–75%**, roughly 50% protein by weight — the same material used to make soy flour, soy protein concentrate/isolate, textured vegetable protein (TVP), tofu, tempeh, soy milk, natto, and miso.
- **Processing loss:** ~1–2%.

**~90–93% of a soybean's mass is technically convertible into human-edible products** with existing food technology. The caveat: feed-grade meal is processed to different specs (mycotoxin tolerances, hexane-residue limits, contamination control) than food-grade meal, so today's feed-grade soy meal can't simply be relabeled as food overnight — but there's no biological barrier. Soy is a genuine human food crop; the ~70% currently routed to livestock feed is a market allocation choice, not a reflection of unsuitability for people.

## 2. Is corn complementary with soy?

Yes — in two distinct ways:

**Agronomically (crop rotation):** Soybeans are legumes that fix atmospheric nitrogen via root-nodule bacteria (~60% of the nitrogen a soybean plant uses comes from biological fixation). Corn is a heavy nitrogen consumer. Rotating the two lets soybean residue replenish soil nitrogen that the following corn crop draws down — farmers following soy with corn can cut nitrogen fertilizer ~30% while still gaining 10–15 bushels/acre in yield. This is the standard US Midwest rotation pattern.

**Nutritionally:** Corn is calorie/carb-dense but its protein is low-quality — deficient in the amino acids lysine and tryptophan. Soy is protein-dense and rich in lysine. Combined, they form a much more complete amino acid profile than either alone. This is exactly why virtually all commercial poultry and swine feed is formulated as corn + soybean meal — it's the standard base ration precisely because of this complementarity.

## 3. The same "redirect to human food" case for corn

- **Global production (2024/25): ~1.23 billion tonnes** — roughly 3x soy's production.
- **Current use (global, approx.):** ~50%+ animal feed, a rapidly growing industrial/ethanol share (dominant in the US, where ~40% of the crop goes to ethanol; globally lower, maybe 12–15%), ~12–15% direct human food, remainder seed/export/other.
- **Calories if 100% redirected to human food:** corn is ~365 kcal/100g → ~1.23 billion tonnes yields **~4.5 quadrillion kcal/year**, against a global need of ~6.9 quadrillion kcal/year. That's **~65% of world caloric needs from corn alone** — far more than soy's ~28%, simply because corn is a calorie staple produced at 3x the volume.
- **Protein if 100% redirected:** ~9g protein/100g → ~111 million tonnes, against a ~150 Mt global need → **~74% of world protein needs by quantity** — though the quality is worse than soy protein due to the lysine/tryptophan deficiency.

### Combine corn + soy (both fully redirected to food)

- **Calories:** ~4.5 + ~1.9 = **~6.4 quadrillion kcal ≈ 93% of global caloric need**
- **Protein:** ~111 + ~154 = **~265 Mt ≈ 176% of global protein need**, and critically, soy's lysine fills exactly the gap corn's protein leaves

Neither crop alone gets you to "feed the world," but together they get remarkably close on calories and comfortably past sufficiency on protein — the same logic that made this pairing the backbone of livestock feed formulation in the first place.

### How much of a corn kernel converts to human-edible food?

Even more than soy — corn has essentially no discarded inedible fraction. The kernel is roughly:
- **Endosperm (~82%):** starch — milled into corn flour, cornmeal, masa, starch, corn syrup
- **Germ (~11%):** oil-rich — pressed into corn oil (already a mainstream cooking oil)
- **Bran/pericarp (~5–6%):** fiber — retained in whole-grain products, used in some fiber ingredients

Unlike soy hulls, corn bran isn't routinely stripped and discarded to animal feed — whole-grain corn products keep it. **Essentially ~95–100% of the kernel by mass is already used in established human food products somewhere in the world** (tortillas, grits, polenta, corn flakes, popcorn, corn oil, HFCS). The "field corn vs. sweet corn" distinction is a red herring — dent/field corn (99% of the US crop) is exactly the variety used to make masa and tortillas across Latin America; it's a product-form choice, not an edibility limit.

## 4. What shortages would occur if all soy and corn were redirected to human consumption

Redirecting 100% of soy and corn to direct human food would free up enormous caloric/protein capacity on paper, but it would simultaneously gut several industries that currently depend on these crops as raw inputs.

### Global animal protein supply — the biggest shock by far

Soybean meal and corn are the backbone of virtually all industrial livestock and aquaculture feed:

- **Pork and poultry:** China alone imports ~105 million tonnes of soybeans/year (over 60% of world soybean trade), with ~95% of that going to soy oil and soybean meal for its pork and poultry industry — the world's largest pork producer/consumer. Cutting that feed source would collapse Chinese (and global) pork and poultry output essentially overnight; there's no fast substitute at that scale.
- **Dairy:** milk yields depend heavily on corn silage + soybean-meal concentrate rations. Without them, per-cow yields would fall sharply (grass-only systems produce far less), causing milk, cheese, and butter shortages.
- **Eggs:** laying hens are fed corn-soy diets almost universally in industrial production; efficient egg-laying rates would drop without them.
- **Aquaculture:** farmed shrimp, tilapia, and salmon — a large and fast-growing share of global seafood — also rely on soy/corn-based feed formulations.

Net effect: meat, dairy, egg, and farmed-seafood shortages and price spikes worldwide, plus knock-on loss of nutrients those foods currently supply cheaply and at scale — vitamin B12, heme iron, and complete essential amino acids (ironic, since soy redirected to humans supplies protein *quantity*, but losing meat/dairy/eggs removes some of the most bioavailable protein/micronutrient sources people currently rely on).

### Liquid fuel supply — ethanol and biodiesel

- **Ethanol:** ~40% of the US corn crop goes to ethanol, blended into most US gasoline (E10/E15) as an octane booster and oxygenate. Removing that overnight would require replacing that blend volume with more petroleum-refined product or other octane boosters — a real fuel-supply and price shock, concentrated in the US and other ethanol-mandate countries (Brazil uses sugarcane instead, so less exposed).
- **Biodiesel/renewable diesel:** soybean oil's share of US biofuel feedstock has grown from under 1% (2001) to roughly 46% of domestically produced soybean oil now going to biofuel, consuming over 1 billion lbs of soybean oil/month. Yanking that would shrink renewable diesel/biodiesel supply just as trucking and aviation (SAF) are leaning on it more.

### Processed-food and industrial inputs

- **High-fructose corn syrup (HFCS) and corn starch:** a huge share of packaged food, soda, and confectionery sweetening and thickening comes from corn. Losing this feedstock would force reformulation toward cane/beet sugar — itself a limited global supply, so sugar prices would spike as a substitution effect.
- **Bioplastics (PLA), corn-based adhesives, pharmaceutical excipients:** all draw on corn starch/derivatives.
- **Soy-based inks, paints, lubricants, cosmetics, candles:** soybean oil is a common industrial feedstock outside food entirely; those industries would face feedstock shortages too.

### Trade and geopolitical disruption

- Countries built around soy/corn *export* to feed markets (Brazil, US, Argentina) would lose major revenue streams tied to feed and biofuel demand, not just food demand.
- Countries built around soy/corn *import* for livestock (China, EU dairy/pork sectors, the Netherlands as a livestock-export hub) would see their agricultural economies destabilized — this isn't a slow adjustment, it's the removal of the input their entire livestock sector is built on.

### The core tradeoff

This is the flip side of the "could soy/corn feed the world" calculation: the reason soy and corn aren't currently used mostly as direct human food isn't inefficiency or waste — it's that they're load-bearing inputs for meat/dairy/egg production, transportation fuel, and packaged-food manufacturing that billions of people also depend on. Redirecting 100% of production to direct food would raise raw calorie/protein availability on paper, but in practice it would trigger simultaneous shortages in meat, dairy, eggs, seafood, transportation fuel, sweeteners, and various industrial goods — a much messier outcome than the direct human-food math alone suggests, and one that would take years of infrastructure and dietary shift (not an overnight switch) to absorb without severe price shocks and scarcity.
